\begin{abstract}
Film production is an economically prominent industry, and both commercial performance and audience reception are crucial to estimate before release. In this term project, as a group we study the TMDB 5000 Movie Data Set, which contains metadata for 4,803 films and covers recorded release dates from 1916 to 2017. Data includes budget, revenue, genres, keywords, runtime, language, popularity, and audience ratings. Our main goals are to use these data to reach conclusions on the success of a potential movie or an upcoming one, investigate genre and keyword co-occurrence through association mining, and explore natural movie groups through clustering. This report presents our initial motivation and goals, reviews the related work of the project, and describes the data set. We identify important data-quality issues: 21.59\% of films have a budget value of zero and 29.71\% have a revenue value of zero, values that are likely to represent missing or unreported financial information in many cases. In addition, audience-derived variables such as \texttt{vote\_average} and \texttt{vote\_count}, and the snapshot-based \texttt{popularity} measure, should not be treated as pre-release predictors of revenue because this would introduce information leakage. Several attributes are stored as JSON-like text and require parsing before analysis.

Our project repository is available at \url{https://github.com/Alpprlk/CE-477-TMDB-Movie}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
As production of a film requires a large investment before release of a movie, outcomes of the project can be used by producers and investors to have a prediction of financial success of the movie prior to release. Also, makes it possible to make a prediction of audience reaction, therefore it has been studied as a decision-support problem for studios, distributors, and exhibitors in earlier studies. Besides revenue, audience ratings are an important indicator of how a film is received and are also useful in recommendation and audience-analysis settings.

We use the TMDB 5000 Movie Data Set, which contains metadata for 4,803 films obtained from The Movie Database (TMDb) and distributed on Kaggle.

During the term, we plan to investigate the following questions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item Which attributes that are available before release, such as budget, runtime, genres, language, and release month, can predict movie revenue? This is a regression problem with \texttt{revenue} as the target.
    \item Can films be classified as highly rated or not from metadata that does not directly encode post-release audience reaction? This is a classification problem based on \texttt{vote\_average}.
    \item Which genres and keywords frequently occur together? We plan to study this with association mining.
    \item Do films form natural groups according to variables such as budget, runtime, genre, and, for descriptive clustering, popularity?
\end{enumerate}

We will distinguish predictive features from descriptive variables. In particular, \texttt{vote\_average} and \texttt{vote\_count} are only observed after audience voting has occurred, while the \texttt{popularity} value in this static data set is a time-dependent snapshot. Using these variables as if they were available before release would make a pre-release revenue model unrealistically optimistic.

The rest of this report is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work. Section III describes the data set, its metadata, statistics, visualizations, and data-quality issues.

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Box-Office Prediction}

Sharda and Delen \cite{sharda2006} formulated box-office prediction as a classification problem. They divided films into nine categories, ranging from flop to blockbuster, and applied neural networks. Their study employed 10-fold cross-validation and reported that neural networks achieved better performance than the statistical techniques used for comparison.

Udandarao and Gupta \cite{udandarao2024}, on the other hand, approached movie revenue prediction as a regression problem and compared linear regression, decision trees, random forest, bagging, XGBoost, and gradient boosting models. Their feature set included genre, MPAA rating, release year, IMDb rating, budget, runtime, and other movie-related information. Their work provides a recent example of applying multiple regression models to movie-revenue prediction. However, some of their features, such as ratings and vote-related information, would not be suitable for a strictly pre-release prediction setting.

\subsection{Studies Based on TMDB Data}

Jalili Torkamani et al. \cite{torkamani2026} analyzed release season, production budget, and financial performance using a larger TMDB dataset. Their random-forest revenue model achieved an $R^2$ value of 0.652, and their analysis showed that production budget and market-interest variables were more influential than release season. Although their dataset differs from the TMDB 5000 sample used in this project, their study motivates a careful investigation of budget and release-related variables.

\subsection{Film Metadata for Recommendation}

Percevali and Manzato \cite{percevali2013} evaluated different types of movie metadata for content-based recommendation using MovieLens and IMDb data. They found that genre information produced the best predictions among the metadata types they tested. This finding supports the use of genres, and possibly keywords, as informative structured features and as items for association mining and clustering.



\subsection{Summary and Positioning}

Previous studies on movie-performance modeling have typically involved production and descriptive metadata, and both linear and ensemble models have appeared in the literature. This project will begin with interpretable baseline models and compare them with stronger ensemble methods. It will also explicitly differentiate between variables that can be used for pre-release prediction and variables that characterize post-release audience response. In addition, suspicious zero financial values will be treated as data-quality issues rather than being assumed to be valid real values.


\section{Data Set Description}
\subsection{Source and Metadata}
The TMDB 5000 Movie Data Set is available on Kaggle and contains
movie metadata from The Movie Database (TMDb) \cite{tmdb,kaggle}.
It consists of two CSV files covering the same 4,803 movies.
The \texttt{tmdb\_5000\_movies.csv} file is approximately 5.7 MB
and contains 20 attributes. The \texttt{tmdb\_5000\_credits.csv}
file is approximately 40.0 MB and contains four attributes:
\texttt{movie\_id}, \texttt{title}, \texttt{cast}, and \texttt{crew}.

The two files can be joined using the \texttt{id} and
\texttt{movie\_id} columns. All 4,803 movie IDs and titles match
between the two files. The release dates range from 4 September
1916 to 3 February 2017, with only one missing release date.
The median release year is 2005, and 72.8\% of the records with
release-year information are from 2000 or later.

The dataset does not clearly state the currency used for budget
and revenue. In this report, we assume that these values are in
US dollars. Since the currency is not explicitly given in the
CSV files, we consider this as a limitation of the dataset.

\subsection{Data Quality}
Explicit missing values. The main explicit missing values occur in homepage (3,091 films; 64.36\%) and tagline (844 films; 17.57\%). In addition, overview is missing for 3 films, runtime for 2, and release date for 1. Figure~\ref{fig:missing} visualizes these counts. Some JSON-like attributes also contain empty lists: 412 films have no keywords, 351 have no production company, 174 have no production country, 86 have no spoken language, and 28 have no genre. In the credits file, 43 films have an empty cast and 28 have an empty crew.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{missing_values.png}
\caption{Explicit missing values in the movies CSV.}
\label{fig:missing}
\end{figure}

Zero and suspicious values. Table~\ref{tab:quality} summarizes numeric values that require additional attention. A budget of zero is recorded for 1,037 films (21.59\%) and revenue is zero for 1,427 films (29.71\%); both are zero for 890 films. These zeros are better treated as potentially missing or unreported financial values unless independently verified, rather than being automatically interpreted as genuine zero-dollar budgets or revenues. Only 3,229 films (67.23\%) have both positive budget and positive revenue. There are also 35 positive budget values and 27 positive revenue values below 10,000; these are unusually small and should be investigated rather than automatically labeled as recording errors. Thirty-five films have runtime equal to zero.


Of the 4,803 films, 62 have no recorded votes and 63 have vote average = 0. Moreover, 411 films (8.56\%) have fewer than 10 votes, so rating reliability varies considerably across films.

Attributes unsuitable for pre-release prediction. Within the 3,229 films with positive budget and revenue, revenue has a Pearson correlation of 0.756 with vote count, 0.705 with budget, and 0.602 with popularity. The large correlations with audience-response variables may improve apparent predictive accuracy, but they do not represent information that can safely be assumed available before release. Therefore, for pre-release revenue prediction we plan to exclude vote average, vote count, and the snapshot-based popularity feature. For pre-release rating prediction, vote count and popularity will likewise be excluded.

Figure~\ref{fig:budgetrevenue} illustrates the relationship between positive budget and revenue values on logarithmic scales. The wide spread around the overall upward trend suggests that budget alone is not sufficient to explain revenue.

Bias and limitations. English is the original language of 93.80\% of the films, so the data set mainly represents English-language cinema. The data end in February 2017. Monetary values are not adjusted for inflation, so budgets and revenues from different decades are not directly comparable. In addition, audience ratings are based on widely varying numbers of votes, from zero to 13,752, which means that the reliability of vote average is not uniform across films.
