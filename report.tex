\begin{abstract}
Film production is an economically prominent industry, and both commercial performance and audience reception are crucial to estimate before release. In this term project, as a group we study the TMDB 5000 Movie Data Set, which contains metadata for 4,803 films and covers recorded release dates from 1916 to 2017. Data includes budget, revenue, genres, keywords, runtime, language, popularity, and audience ratings. Our main goals are to use these data to reach conclusions on the success of a potential movie or an upcoming one, investigate genre and keyword co-occurrence through association mining, and explore natural movie groups through clustering. This report presents our initial motivation and goals, reviews the related work of the project, and describes the data set. We identify important data-quality issues: 21.59\% of films have a budget value of zero and 29.71\% have a revenue value of zero, values that are likely to represent missing or unreported financial information in many cases. In addition, audience-derived variables such as \texttt{vote\_average} and \texttt{vote\_count}, and the snapshot-based \texttt{popularity} measure, should not be treated as pre-release predictors of revenue because this would introduce information leakage. Several attributes are stored as JSON-like text and require parsing before analysis.

Our project repository is available at \url{https://github.com/Alpprlk/CE-477-TMDB-Movie}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Making a film requires a large investment long before anyone knows how the audience will react, so a wrong estimate is costly for several groups. Producers and investors need an early idea of a film's financial success to decide which projects to fund and how large a budget to commit. Distributors and exhibitors use such estimates to plan release dates and the number of screens, and streaming platforms use them when they choose which titles to acquire. For this reason, predicting financial outcome and audience reaction before release has been studied as a decision-support problem in earlier work. Besides revenue, audience ratings show how a film is received and are also useful in recommendation and audience-analysis settings.

We use the TMDB 5000 Movie Data Set, which contains metadata for 4,803 films obtained from The Movie Database (TMDb) and distributed on Kaggle.

During the term, we plan to investigate the following questions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item Which attributes that are available before release, such as budget, runtime, genres, language, and release month, can predict movie revenue? This is a regression problem with \texttt{revenue} as the target.
    \item Can films be classified as highly rated or not from metadata that does not directly encode post-release audience reaction? This is a classification problem based on \texttt{vote\_average}.
    \item Which genres and keywords frequently occur together? We plan to study this with association mining.
    \item Do films form natural groups according to variables such as budget, runtime, genre, and, for descriptive clustering, popularity?
\end{enumerate}
From these questions we derive four hypotheses, which are examined in the remainder of this report:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{H\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,nosep]
   \item Among the attributes available before release, budget is the strongest single explanatory variable for revenue.
    \item Budget, revenue, popularity, and vote count are strongly right-skewed, and most of their outliers are genuine blockbusters rather than recording errors.
   \item Audience variables such as vote count and popularity correlate with revenue about as strongly as budget does, but they are not available before release and must be excluded to avoid information leakage.
   \item The data set is biased toward English-language films and toward recent releases, so a model trained on it mainly reflects recent English-language cinema.
\end{enumerate}
We will distinguish predictive features from descriptive variables. In particular, \texttt{vote\_average} and \texttt{vote\_count} are only observed after audience voting has occurred, while the \texttt{popularity} value in this static data set is a time-dependent snapshot. Using these variables as if they were available before release would make a pre-release revenue model unrealistically optimistic.

The rest of this report is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data set, its metadata, statistics, categorical and temporal attributes, data-quality issues, numeric distributions, and scatter plots. Section~\ref{sec:prep} describes the preprocessing steps: missing-value handling, outlier detection, discretization, correlation analysis, attribute selection, scaling, encoding, principal component analysis, data augmentation, and imputation.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Box-Office Prediction}

Sharda and Delen \cite{sharda2006} formulated box-office prediction as a classification problem. They divided films into nine categories, ranging from flop to blockbuster, and applied neural networks. Their study employed 10-fold cross-validation and reported that neural networks achieved better performance than the statistical techniques used for comparison.

Udandarao and Gupta \cite{udandarao2024}, on the other hand, approached movie revenue prediction as a regression problem and compared linear regression, decision trees, random forest, bagging, XGBoost, and gradient boosting models. Their feature set included genre, MPAA rating, release year, IMDb rating, budget, runtime, and other movie-related information. Their work provides a recent example of applying multiple regression models to movie-revenue prediction. However, some of their features, such as ratings and vote-related information, would not be suitable for a strictly pre-release prediction setting.

\subsection{Studies Based on TMDB Data}

Jalili Torkamani et al. \cite{torkamani2026} analyzed release season, production budget, and financial performance using a larger TMDB dataset. Their random-forest revenue model achieved an $R^2$ value of 0.652, and their analysis showed that production budget and market-interest variables were more influential than release season. Although their dataset differs from the TMDB 5000 sample used in this project, their study motivates a careful investigation of budget and release-related variables.

\subsection{Film Metadata for Recommendation}

Percevali and Manzato \cite{percevali2013} evaluated different types of movie metadata for content-based recommendation using MovieLens and IMDb data. They found that genre information produced the best predictions among the metadata types they tested. This finding supports the use of genres, and possibly keywords, as informative structured features and as items for association mining and clustering.



\subsection{Summary and Positioning}

Previous studies on movie-performance modeling have typically involved production and descriptive metadata, and both linear and ensemble models have appeared in the literature. This project will begin with interpretable baseline models and compare them with stronger ensemble methods. It will also explicitly differentiate between variables that can be used for pre-release prediction and variables that characterize post-release audience response. In addition, suspicious zero financial values will be treated as data-quality issues rather than being assumed to be valid real values.


\section{Data Set Description}\label{sec:data}
\subsection{Source and Metadata}
The TMDB 5000 Movie Data Set is available on Kaggle and contains
movie metadata from The Movie Database (TMDb) \cite{tmdb,kaggle}.
It consists of two CSV files covering the same 4,803 movies.
The \texttt{tmdb\_5000\_movies.csv} file is approximately 5.7 MB
and contains 20 attributes. The \texttt{tmdb\_5000\_credits.csv}
file is approximately 40.0 MB and contains four attributes:
\texttt{movie\_id}, \texttt{title}, \texttt{cast}, and \texttt{crew}.

The two files can be joined using the \texttt{id} and
\texttt{movie\_id} columns. All 4,803 movie IDs and titles match
between the two files. The release dates range from 4 September
1916 to 3 February 2017, with only one missing release date.
The median release year is 2005, and 72.8\% of the records with
release-year information are from 2000 or later.

The dataset does not clearly state the currency used for budget
and revenue. In this report, we assume that these values are in
US dollars. Since the currency is not explicitly given in the
CSV files, we consider this as a limitation of the dataset.

\subsection{Data Quality}
\label{sec:data_quality}
Explicit missing values. The main explicit missing values occur in homepage (3,091 films; 64.36\%) and tagline (844 films; 17.57\%). In addition, overview is missing for 3 films, runtime for 2, and release date for 1. Figure~\ref{fig:missing} visualizes these counts for the 20 original attributes; attributes without missing values are not shown. Some JSON-like attributes also contain empty lists: 412 films have no keywords, 351 have no production company, 174 have no production country, 86 have no spoken language, and 28 have no genre. In the credits file, 43 films have an empty cast and 28 have an empty crew.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{missing_values.png}
\caption{Original attributes of the movies CSV that contain missing values.}

Zero and suspicious values. Table~\ref{tab:quality} summarizes numeric values that require additional attention. A budget of zero is recorded for 1,037 films (21.59\%) and revenue is zero for 1,427 films (29.71\%); both are zero for 890 films. These zeros are better treated as potentially missing or unreported financial values unless independently verified, rather than being automatically interpreted as genuine zero-dollar budgets or revenues. Only 3,229 films (67.23\%) have both positive budget and positive revenue. There are also 35 positive budget values and 27 positive revenue values below 10,000; these are unusually small and should be investigated rather than automatically labeled as recording errors. Thirty-five films have runtime equal to zero.


Of the 4,803 films, 62 have no recorded votes and 63 have vote average = 0. Moreover, 411 films (8.56\%) have fewer than 10 votes, so rating reliability varies considerably across films.

Attributes unsuitable for pre-release prediction. Within the 3,229 films with positive budget and revenue, revenue has a Pearson correlation of 0.756 with vote count, 0.705 with budget, and 0.602 with popularity. The large correlations with audience-response variables may improve apparent predictive accuracy, but they do not represent information that can safely be assumed available before release. Therefore, for pre-release revenue prediction we plan to exclude vote average, vote count, and the snapshot-based popularity feature. For pre-release rating prediction, vote count and popularity will likewise be excluded.


Bias and limitations. English is the original language of 93.80\% of the films, so the data set mainly represents English-language cinema. The data end in February 2017. Monetary values are not adjusted for inflation, so budgets and revenues from different decades are not directly comparable. In addition, audience ratings are based on widely varying numbers of votes, from zero to 13,752, which means that the reliability of vote average is not uniform across films.

\textit{Financial attributes.}
Budget and revenue are strongly right-skewed. In the full data the mean
budget (29.05M) is almost twice the median (15.00M), and the mean revenue
(82.26M) is more than four times the median (19.17M), with the revenue
maximum reaching 2,787.97M (Table~\ref{tab:numeric}). The skewness is 2.44 for
budget and 4.44 for revenue, and 6.7\% and 9.8\% of the films are flagged
as outliers by the 1.5$\times$IQR rule (Figure~\ref{fig:box_all}). These
outliers are mostly genuine blockbusters rather than errors, so we do not
plan to delete them; instead we will use a log transform for modeling.
After a $\log_{10}$ transform (Figure~\ref{fig:hist}) the positive budgets
and revenues look much closer to a symmetric distribution, which supports
this choice. The box plot of the full data is also visibly affected by the
1,037 zero budgets and 1,427 zero revenues: the lower quartile of revenue
is exactly 0, because more than a quarter of the films have a revenue of
zero, and the lower quartile of budget is only 0.79M. These zeros are
probably missing rather than real values. Restricting the data to positive
records (Figure~\ref{fig:box_pos}) raises the median revenue from 19.17M to
55.18M and the median budget from 15.00M to 25.00M, which shows how
strongly the zeros influence any statistic computed on the raw columns.
Even in the positive subset, the skewness remains 2.09 for budget and
3.82 for revenue, so the log transform is still needed. In log space
(Figure~\ref{fig:box_pos}) the few extremely small values (budgets and
revenues of only a few dollars) appear as isolated low outliers, which
matches the suspicious values reported in Table~\ref{tab:quality}.
 
\textit{Runtime.}
Runtime is the most symmetric attribute (mean 106.88, median 103.00 min,
skewness 0.72). Half of the films last between 94 and 118 minutes
(Q1--Q3), and 3.8\% of the films are flagged as outliers: very short
films, the 35 films with runtime zero, and a small number of very long
films, up to 338 minutes. The zero runtimes are treated as missing values
rather than true durations. Among films with positive budget and revenue,
the skewness rises to 1.69 because the very short films are removed while
the long films remain.
 
\textit{Audience attributes.}
Vote average is concentrated around 6--7 (Q1--Q3 of 5.6--6.8, median
6.20) and is left-skewed (skewness $-1.96$): a handful of films have
values near 0, and these are almost all films with zero or very few votes
(63 films with average zero, 411 films with fewer than 10 votes). Once
these films are removed, the skewness in the positive subset falls to
$-0.61$ and the histogram in Figure~\ref{fig:hist} is approximately
bell-shaped with a peak near 6.3. Popularity (median 12.92, mean 21.49,
maximum 875.58, skewness 9.72) and vote count (median 235, mean 690.22,
maximum 13,752, skewness 3.82) are heavily right-skewed, with 5.7\% and
10.5\% of films flagged as outliers. A few very popular films dominate the
upper tail, so a log transform is again appropriate.
 
\textit{Release year.}
The histogram of release years is strongly left-skewed: only a small
number of films come from before 1980, whereas most films are from the
2000s, consistent with the 72.8\% share of films released in 2000 or
later. Any model trained on this data will therefore mainly learn the
behavior of recent films.
 
\textit{Implication for preprocessing.}
In summary, almost all numeric attributes except runtime are heavily
skewed, contain many legitimate extreme values, and contain zeros that
behave like missing data. We will therefore (i) treat zero budget,
revenue and runtime as missing, (ii) apply log transforms to budget,
revenue, popularity and vote count, and (iii) prefer robust statistics
(median, IQR) over mean and standard deviation when describing them.

logarithmic scales of Figure~\ref{fig:sc_budget}(b) the pattern is much
clearer: there is a positive, roughly linear trend, with a log--log
Pearson correlation of 0.657 and a fitted slope of 0.82 ($R^2 = 0.43$).
Taken at face value, a tenfold increase in budget is associated with a
$10^{0.82}\approx 6.6$-fold increase in revenue, i.e., revenue grows
slightly less than proportionally with budget; part of this flattening may
come from excluding the zero-valued records. The Pearson correlation on
the original scale is 0.705 and the Spearman rank correlation is 0.679,
which indicates that the relationship is monotonic but far from perfect.
The wide vertical scatter around the trend line shows that budget alone
does not determine revenue: 24.4\% of the films earn less than their
budget, whereas 8.4\% earn more than ten times their budget, and the
median revenue-to-budget ratio is 2.30. High-budget films carry a larger
risk of loss but also include the biggest successes, such as the
highest-grossing films labeled in Figure~\ref{fig:sc_budget}(a). Note also
the small group of points with very small budgets or revenues (below
10,000 USD) in the lower left part of Figure~\ref{fig:sc_budget}(b); these
may be recording errors and will be handled in the outlier-detection
step.
 
\textit{Vote count versus revenue.}
Vote count and revenue are also strongly related (Figure~\ref{fig:sc_votes}).
Among the 3,374 films with positive vote count and revenue, the Pearson
correlation is 0.758 (0.756 when restricted to the 3,229 films that
also have a positive budget, as in Section~\ref{sec:data_quality}) and the Spearman correlation is 0.749, both higher
than for budget. On the log--log scale, however, the correlation is 0.656
with a slope of 0.96 and $R^2 = 0.43$, essentially the same as for budget
(0.657). The higher raw correlation is therefore partly driven by a small
number of blockbusters that have both extreme vote counts and extreme
revenues. The slope close to one suggests that revenue grows roughly in
proportion to the number of votes, which is expected because both
quantities increase with the number of people who watch a film. However,
the vote count is accumulated after release and is partly a consequence of
the revenue. We therefore use this plot only to understand the data and
will \emph{not} use vote count as a predictor in the pre-release revenue
model, in order to avoid information leakage.
 
\textit{Summary.}
Both relationships are clearly positive but non-linear on the original
scale and much more linear after a log transform. Budget is the most
important legitimate pre-release numeric predictor identified so far,
but it explains only about 43\% of the variance of log revenue
($R^2 = 0.43$) on its own. This suggests that other attributes (genre,
release month, language, production company) are needed, which motivates
the models planned in the following milestones.
\section{Preprocessing}\label{sec:prep}

\subsection{Missing-Value Handling}

The initial data-quality analysis showed that \texttt{runtime} contains
2 explicitly missing values and 35 records with a runtime of zero.
Since a zero-minute runtime is not considered a meaningful movie
duration, these zero values were treated as missing values. Therefore,
37 runtime values required treatment.

The median was preferred for imputation because it is less sensitive
to unusually short or long films than the mean. The median calculated
from valid positive runtime values was 104 minutes. The 37 missing or
invalid runtime values were therefore replaced with 104 minutes.
After imputation, the cleaned runtime attribute contained no missing
values.

Budget and revenue were handled differently. The dataset contains
1,037 zero budget values and 1,427 zero revenue values. Since these
values may represent missing or unreported financial information, they
were marked as unknown in the cleaned versions of the attributes.
They were not replaced with the mean or median because doing so would
introduce artificial financial values into the dataset.


\subsection{Outlier Detection}

Outliers in budget, revenue, and runtime were detected using the
1.5$\times$IQR rule. Before calculating the quartiles, zero budget and
revenue values were excluded because they had already been treated as
potentially missing or unreported. Missing and zero runtime values were
also excluded before runtime outlier detection.

For each attribute, the first quartile $Q_1$, third quartile $Q_3$, and
interquartile range were calculated as

\[
IQR = Q_3 - Q_1.
\]

An observation was flagged as a suspected outlier if it was below

\[
Q_1 - 1.5 \times IQR
\]

or above

\[
Q_3 + 1.5 \times IQR.
\]

Table~\ref{tab:cleaning_outliers} summarizes the resulting limits and
outlier counts.

\subsection{Correlation Analysis}
Budget and revenue are left unknown rather than imputed in the cleaning steps above, so the
remaining analyses use the 3,229 films with positive budget and revenue. Figure~\ref{fig:p4_corr}
shows the Pearson correlation matrix of nine attributes available before release, three
audience attributes that are only known after release, and the target $\log_{10}$ revenue.
Budget, popularity and vote count enter on a $\log_{10}$ scale because of their skewness
(see the numeric distributions above); the added attributes are counts taken from the JSON
columns (numbers of genres, keywords, production companies, production countries, and cast members)
and the release month.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{fig4_corr.png}
  \caption{Pearson correlation matrix for the 3,229 films with positive budget and revenue.
  The first block contains the nine attributes available before release, the second block
  the three post-release audience attributes, and the last row/column the target
  $\log_{10}$ revenue.}
  \label{fig:p4_corr}

Among the pre-release attributes, $\log_{10}$ budget has by far the strongest linear relation
with $\log_{10}$ revenue ($r=0.66$, identical to the log--log value reported in the scatter plot
section; on the raw scale the correlation is 0.705). Cast size ($r=0.28$), number of keywords (0.19)
and runtime (0.19) are weaker, and release month (0.02) and number of production countries
(0.03) show almost no linear relation. The post-release attributes are as strongly correlated with
revenue as budget is (vote count 0.67, popularity 0.63), but this is exactly the leakage problem
discussed earlier: these quantities grow with the size of the audience, which also produces the revenue.
Popularity and vote count are also nearly redundant with each other ($r=0.92$). Among the nine
pre-release attributes no pair exceeds $|r|=0.8$; the highest value is 0.46, between the
numbers of production companies and production countries. Multicollinearity is therefore not a
problem for the pre-release set. Pearson correlation only measures linear association, so
attributes with a non-linear effect (such as the release month) can look unimportant here;
this is why mutual information is used in the next step.

\subsection{Attribute Selection}
Attribute selection was done in three steps. First, a domain rule removed everything that is not
known before release: \texttt{popularity}, \texttt{vote\_average} and \texttt{vote\_count}.
Identifiers and free text (\texttt{id}, titles, \texttt{homepage}, \texttt{tagline},
\texttt{overview}) were not used as features, and \texttt{status} was dropped because 3,228
of the 3,229 films are ``Released''. Second, a redundancy filter was set to remove one attribute
of every pair with $|r|>0.8$; no pair qualified (Figure~\ref{fig:p4_corr}). Third, the nine
remaining attributes were ranked by their mutual information (MI) with $\log_{10}$ revenue,
averaged over five random seeds (Figure~\ref{fig:p4_sel}). To decide which MI values mean
anything, the target was shuffled ten times and the largest MI obtained from the shuffled
target (0.037) was used as the noise level.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{fig4_selection.png}
  \caption{Mutual information between each attribute and $\log_{10}$ revenue. The dashed line is
  the noise level obtained with a shuffled target. The three post-release attributes
  (orange) are removed by the domain rule even though they rank high.}
  \label{fig:p4_sel}
\end{figure}

Four pre-release attributes are above the noise level: budget (MI = 0.36), cast size (0.10), release
month (0.08) and number of production companies (0.04). The release month is notable, since its
Pearson correlation is only 0.02; the MI suggests a non-linear (probably seasonal) effect, although
with 3,229 films this estimate is noisy. The other five attributes (runtime, release year, number of
genres, keywords and production countries) are within the noise level individually.

To check whether a strict filter is a good idea, we compared feature sets with a five-fold
cross-validated $R^2$ for $\log_{10}$ revenue using ridge regression and a random forest
(Table~\ref{tab:p4_sel}). Budget alone gives $R^2\approx0.41$--$0.42$. All nine pre-release attributes
reach 0.47 (ridge) and 0.49 (random forest), whereas the strict MI filter with four attributes reaches only 0.45
and 0.44. Adding the three post-release attributes raises $R^2$ to about 0.64, a jump of 0.15--0.17 that
would make a pre-release model look much better than it really is. We therefore keep all nine pre-release attributes
as the final feature set: MI looks at each attribute alone and misses their joint contribution, and the
filter would cost 0.02--0.05 of $R^2$. The differences between the first three rows
are of the same size as the fold-to-fold standard deviation (about 0.08), so they should be read as a tendency
only. MI is used as a ranking, and embedded selection (e.g., L1 or forest importance) can be tried again
when the models are built. One caveat is that the keyword count may include tags added by users after release.

\subsection{Normalization and Standardization}
Two rescalings were applied to the numeric attributes: min--max normalization,
$x'=(x-x_{\min})/(x_{\max}-x_{\min})$, which maps values to $[0,1]$, and z-score standardization,
$z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$, which gives mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Both are linear and do not change the
shape of a distribution; only the $\log_{10}$ transform does. Table~\ref{tab:p4_skew} shows its effect
on the skewness of the four skewed attributes, and Figure~\ref{fig:p4_scaling} compares the two
rescalings on budget with and without the log transform.

On the raw scale (Figure~\ref{fig:p4_scaling}a), min--max normalization is dominated by a few very expensive
films: 63.3\% of the films are squeezed below 0.1 and the median is 0.07, so almost all films are
nearly indistinguishable for a distance-based method. After the log transform (Figure~\ref{fig:p4_scaling}b) the values
are spread over the interval (median 0.86), but now the mass sits near 1 and a thin tail extends to the left.
For z-scores, 76 films have $|z|>3$ on the raw scale (maximum 7.6) and 34 after the log transform.
The log transform also changes the direction of the skew: the skewness of budget goes from 2.09 to $-3.16$
(Table~\ref{tab:p4_skew}). This is not a failure of the transform; it comes from the suspiciously small
values reported in the data-quality section, such as budgets of a few dollars, which become extreme on a
log scale (minimum $z=-10.1$). When the 24 films with a budget or revenue below 10,000 USD are excluded as a
sensitivity check, the skewness of $\log_{10}$ budget and revenue becomes $-1.15$ and $-1.14$ and the minimum
$z$ is $-5.5$. The group should therefore decide whether these values are treated as unknown, as is done for
zeros; we did not remove them here to stay consistent with the cleaning above.

For the later models we use $\log_{10}$ followed by z-score standardization for methods that depend on variance
or distance (ridge regression, PCA, k-NN, SMOTE). Min--max normalization is kept for methods that need a
bounded input range, and tree-based models need no scaling. To avoid leaking test information, the scaler
parameters must be computed on the training part only; all cross-validated experiments in this report fit the scaler
inside each training fold. Only the PCA illustration below scales all films at once, which is acceptable
because no target information is used there.

\subsection{Encoding of Categorical Attributes}

The original language has 37 values in the full data set but is extremely concentrated: English accounts for

The components can be interpreted through their loadings. PC1 is a production-scale axis: the number of production companies (0.47),

which shows that PCA, being variance-based, is sensitive to the suspicious values discussed above.

\subsection{Data Augmentation}
Augmentation is only useful if a class is rare. Revenue is a numeric target, so to demonstrate augmentation we defined a
binary label ``blockbuster'' (revenue of at least 300 million USD, not adjusted for inflation). It applies to 345 of the 3,229 films (10.7\%), so
the classes are imbalanced. We oversampled the minority class with SMOTE \cite{chawla2002smote}, which creates synthetic films by interpolating between a blockbuster and
one of its five nearest blockbuster neighbors, using the nine numeric attributes. SMOTE was compared with a baseline and with class weighting
(a way to compensate for imbalance without synthetic data) for logistic regression and a random forest, using 5-fold stratified cross-validation repeated three times. To avoid leakage, SMOTE was applied
inside each training fold only, so no synthetic sample ever reaches a test fold. Figure~\ref{fig:p4_smote} illustrates the procedure on the whole data set and Table~\ref{tab:p4_aug}
gives the cross-validated results.

SMOTE changes the behavior of both models in the same way (Table~\ref{tab:p4_aug}): recall of the blockbuster class rises (0.40 to 0.84 for logistic
regression, 0.47 to 0.70 for the random forest) while precision falls (0.76 to 0.35 and 0.73 to 0.56). Whether this is an improvement depends on the goal.
The F1 score drops slightly for logistic regression (0.52 to 0.49) and rises for the random forest (0.57 to 0.62), but the gain of about 0.05 is
roughly one fold-to-fold standard deviation (0.04--0.05), so it is not conclusive. The ROC AUC does not change (0.905 to 0.907 and 0.915 to 0.918): the models do not rank films better,
they only use a more liberal decision threshold. In fact, plain class weighting reaches the same results as SMOTE (random forest F1 0.62 for both),
so the synthetic films bring no benefit over reweighting. This is understandable because SMOTE only interpolates between existing blockbusters
(Figure~\ref{fig:p4_smote}b); the new points lie in the region the real blockbusters already occupy and add no new information. SMOTE also has limitations for this
data: it creates non-integer values for counts and months, and it is not meaningful for one-hot columns, which is why only the numeric attributes
were used (SMOTE-NC would be needed for the mixed matrix). Other common augmentation techniques, such as adding noise or text paraphrasing of the \texttt{overview} field,
are not meaningful for these tabular metadata. We conclude that augmentation can be used to raise recall for rare, high-revenue films, but
that threshold tuning or class weights should be tried first.

\subsection{Imputation of Missing Budgets}
The cleaning steps above left zero budgets as unknown, but the assignment also asks for imputation, so we tested it for
the budget attribute. Revenue is the target and is never imputed. Of the 4,803 films, 1,037 have an unknown budget; for 890 of them the revenue
is unknown too, which leaves 147 films with a known revenue but an unknown budget. Imputing their budgets would raise the number of usable films
from 3,229 to 3,376 (+4.6\%). The imputation uses only attributes known before release (runtime, release year and month, the counts of genres, production
companies, production countries and cast members, and the genre columns). Revenue is deliberately not used as an input, because that
would move information about the target into a feature.

To choose a method we ran a masking test: 20\% of the 3,731 known budgets of at least 10,000 USD were hidden at random, imputed, and compared
with the true values, repeated ten times. Four methods were compared on $\log_{10}$ budget: the mean, the median, the median within the release decade, and
a $k$-nearest-neighbor imputer ($k=5$) on the standardized inputs. Figure~\ref{fig:p4_imp}a shows the root mean squared error (RMSE).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{fig4_imputation.png}
  \caption{(a) RMSE of $\log_{10}$ budget in the masking test for four imputation methods (mean $\pm$ std over ten repetitions). (b) Distribution of
  the observed budgets and of the 147 budgets imputed with KNN.}
  \label{fig:p4_imp}
\end{figure}

KNN is the best method: RMSE 0.51 against 0.63 for the mean, 0.64 for the median and 0.61 for the decade median (mean absolute error 0.38 against 0.48, 0.47 and 0.44).
An error of 0.51 in $\log_{10}$ units still means a typical factor of about $10^{0.51}\approx3.3$ between the imputed and the true budget, so the
improvement over the mean (about 18\%) is real but modest; the metadata describe the size of a production only roughly. Figure~\ref{fig:p4_imp}b compares the imputed budgets with the observed ones:
the imputed median is 14.9M USD against 24.0M USD for the observed budgets (quartiles 6.4/14.9/25.1M against 8.8/24.0/50.0M). The masking test assumes that budgets are missing at random,
and this result suggests they are not: the films with an unknown budget look like smaller productions in their other attributes. The true imputation error is therefore probably larger than in the
test. For this reason the 3,229 complete films remain the main data for all analyses above, and the 147 imputed films are kept as an optional extension, to be checked in the modeling stage by comparing models trained with
and without them.
